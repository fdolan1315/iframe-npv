# Knowledge Base — Epidemiology & the Eastern-US Center-of-Excellence Target Map

> **Purpose:** prevalence, geographic/commercial concentration, and the actual Eastern-US target institutions for ecopipam (pediatric Tourette syndrome). Reference doc for the interview agent and the candidate.
> **Compiled July 2026.** Figures are parent-report/claims-based unless noted; limitation flags included where sources conflict.

---

## 1. Prevalence & epidemiology — numbers to know cold

| Metric | Figure | Source basis |
|---|---|---|
| **Diagnosed TS**, children 3–17 | **~1 in 333 (0.3%)** ≈ **~174,000 US children** (2016–2019) | CDC, parent-reported NSCH |
| **True prevalence** (diagnosed + undiagnosed) | **~1 in 162 (0.6%)** | Community-screening studies |
| **Undiagnosed share** | **~50%** of children who have TS are not formally diagnosed | Gap between the two figures |
| **All ages, TS (any severity)** | **350,000–450,000** US children *and* adults | 2022 estimate on 2020 Census |
| **All ages, other persistent (chronic) tic disorders** | **~1,000,000** US children and adults | Same 2022 estimate |

- **Core narrative:** ~half of children who actually have TS have never been formally diagnosed. The billable market is *diagnosed* prevalence today, but there is a large latent, under-identified pool — which is exactly why concentrating on high-diagnosis, specialist-dense geographies matters.
- **Sex:** strong male predominance, **~3:1** (CDC parent-report; literature ranges 2:1–4:1). ~3 of every 4 diagnosed pediatric TS patients are boys.
- **Age:** onset typically **5–7**, severity **peaks ~10–12**, tics often attenuate through late adolescence. Diagnosed prevalence is ~2x higher in ages 12–17 vs 6–11; the 11–17 band carries ~74% of cases. This is the sweet-spot age for a pediatric TS therapeutic.
- **Comorbidity load (critical — TS is rarely isolated):** CDC reports **~86%** of children with TS have ≥1 additional mental/behavioral/developmental disorder. Among children with TS: **ADHD ~49%** (vs ~10% without TS), **anxiety ~60%** (vs ~11%), **autism spectrum ~21%** (vs ~3%), plus high OCD and conduct rates. **Implication:** TS patients cluster in child neurology + child psychiatry + developmental-behavioral pediatrics, not general pediatrics.

## 2. Geographic / regional concentration — handle with care

Hard, published **region-by-region TS prevalence** data is thin. **Do not assert a raw "the Northeast has more TS" biology claim in the interview** — it is unsupported. The defensible framing:

- **Diagnosed** prevalence is **access- and demographic-driven**, which functions as de facto geographic concentration. Diagnosis is ~2x more likely in non-Hispanic White children (2009 MMWR); higher-income households show different odds in recent NSCH analysis. **Where the specialists and academic centers are, the diagnosed patients are.**
- **State-level diagnosed-prevalence estimates** are obtainable via the NSCH interactive query system (Data Resource Center / nschdata.org) if the candidate wants a state-by-state overlay for the East.
- **Territory-design takeaway:** commercial concentration for a pediatric TS orphan drug is **not** evenly distributed by population — it concentrates around academic child-neurology/movement-disorder hubs, the TAA Centers of Excellence (§3), and high-volume children's hospitals (§4). Referral geography, not raw census population, is the real map.

## 3. TAA Centers of Excellence — the Eastern target map

**What it is:** the Tourette Association of America (TAA) **Centers of Excellence (CofE)** program, launched 2014, designates premier institutions delivering the highest level of TS care plus research, professional education, and advocacy. As of **February 2025 the TAA recognized ~28 CofEs nationally** (a 2025 round added four sites). These centers concentrate diagnosed patients, KOLs, and the physicians most likely to be early adopters of a novel agent like ecopipam — the **highest-value, highest-density targets**.

## 4. Commercial concentration — high-volume treaters beyond the CofEs (East)

Major children's hospitals' **pediatric movement-disorder / child-neurology and child-psychiatry programs** are where diagnosed TS volume concentrates. Strong Eastern targets even without CofE status:

- **Children's Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP)** — one of the largest pediatric movement-disorder teams in the US; dedicated TS program; **not currently a formal CofE** but very high volume (Mid-Atlantic).
- **Boston Children's Hospital — Tic Disorders & TS Program** (dir. Kinga Tomczak, MD, PhD) — high-volume; pairs with MGH to make Boston a dense metro.
- **Children's National Hospital** (Washington, DC) — serves the DC/Maryland/Virginia corridor.
- CofE children's hospitals (Kennedy Krieger, Nationwide, Cincinnati, Golisano/Rochester, Children's of Alabama, Le Bonheur, Arkansas Children's, Vanderbilt Children's) double as the region's highest-volume sites.

**Targeting logic for the ABD:** the buying center is a **triad** — pediatric/child **neurologists** (esp. movement-disorder-trained), child & adolescent **psychiatrists**, and **developmental-behavioral pediatricians** — concentrated in the **Boston–New York–Philadelphia–Baltimore/DC corridor** plus the **Atlanta / Florida / Nashville / Birmingham** SE cluster. These metros hold both the CofEs and the tertiary children's hospitals, and therefore the bulk of diagnosed, treatment-seeking, medication-eligible pediatric TS patients in the East.

## 5. Payer / demographic notes — pediatric orphan-drug access

- **Public insurance dominates pediatrics:** ~49% of all US children (~37M) were on Medicaid/CHIP as of Oct 2024; >40% of children under 6. For a pediatric launch, roughly **half the addressable base sits in Medicaid/CHIP** — often higher in the neuro/behavioral pediatric population.
- **Heavy state variation (directly relevant to Eastern access strategy):** child Medicaid/CHIP share ranges ~18% (Utah) to ~71% (New Mexico). High-Medicaid Eastern states include **Louisiana (63%), Kentucky (59%), New York (59%), North Carolina (57%)**, plus elevated WV, TN, AL, AR. **Access will be won or lost state Medicaid program by state Medicaid program** — preferred-drug lists, prior authorization, and managed-Medicaid carve-outs will gate more than commercial formularies in much of the SE and in NY.
- **Orphan/small-population dynamics:** with ~174,000 *diagnosed* pediatric patients and a first-line landscape of older antipsychotics/alpha-agonists (mostly off-label), ecopipam enters as a first-in-class, small-population pediatric agent — expect PA/step-through, YGTSS documentation, specialist-prescribing requirements, and reliance on hub/copay and patient-assistance programs. Concentration of prescribing in CofEs is an **access advantage** — payers approve more readily when a designated specialist drives the diagnosis and script.
